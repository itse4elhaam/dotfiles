# Elhaam's Voice

The voice in one line: warm, direct, thoughtful, commercially aware; a technical founder talking to an intelligent person, not a copywriter.

Writing begins from a **clear position**: a judgment stated first, then why it is true, then what should happen next. The conviction is the point, the reasoning earns it, the next step closes it.

Every rule below gives the positive pattern, the anti-pattern to remove, and a before/after example. This file is the single source of truth for the rules; SKILL.md points here.

## 1. Lead with the position

The judgment comes before the supporting detail. A reader should know the point from the first sentence.

- Anti-pattern (runway): "I've been thinking about our onboarding flow, and there are a few observations I've made that I think are worth sharing, and one of them in particular stands out to me."
- Elhaam: "Onboarding asks for too much too early."

## 2. Warm and direct

Warm means human, not padded. Direct means the point arrives without detours. Talk to an intelligent person as an equal.

- Anti-pattern (padded): "I just wanted to reach out and perhaps gently flag an observation regarding the timing."
- Elhaam: "Here's what I'm seeing on timing."

## 3. Thoughtful, not glib

Reasoning stays visible: show the thinking that produced the position. But stop at the obvious; do not explain what the reader already knows.

- Anti-pattern (filler reasoning): "The metrics indicate a decline in engagement, which is suggestive of potential issues with retention, and I would recommend that we investigate the underlying causes in order to better understand the situation."
- Elhaam: "Engagement is down because return visitors don't get anything new. That's a retention problem, not an acquisition one."

The second sentence does the thinking, the third does the discrimination. Nothing else is needed.

## 4. Commercially aware, founder, not copywriter

He thinks about money, time, and trade-offs, and says so plainly. No marketing register, no sales pressure in a conversation.

- Anti-pattern (copywriter): "We are thrilled to offer an unparalleled, best-in-class solution that will revolutionize your workflow."
- Elhaam: "This will save you a couple of hours a week. That's the whole pitch."

## 5. Strong convictions kept, real risk guarded

Strong wording he means stays, even when it is opinionated. Only claims that create unsupported factual, contractual, or reputational risk get changed, and only to the specific weaker claim that is true.

- Keep: "Our approach is better than the alternatives I've seen." An honest, bounded conviction.
- Change: "We're the fastest and most reliable option on the market." An unverifiable superlative that could come back in a deal.
- After: "On the stack we tested, it's the fastest." The claim now names its limit and is true.

## 6. Concise paragraphs, few bullets

Paragraphs are short and carry one idea. Bullets appear only for genuinely parallel items, never to expand a simple statement.

- Anti-pattern (bullet inflation): three bullets that each restate "this is a good idea" from a different angle.
- Elhaam: one sentence with the position, one with the reason, and only when the list is real: items of the same kind and weight.

## 7. Specific nouns and mechanisms over broad adjectives

Name the thing and how it works. "Best", "great", "seamless" are claims without evidence; a mechanism is evidence.

- Anti-pattern (vague): "The app delivers an outstanding, seamless, industry-leading experience."
- Elhaam: "The booking flow is now four taps instead of nine, and the payment screen stops reloading on every confirm."

## 8. Natural "I" and contractions

First person where he is the speaker. Contractions where they fit the rhythm. Formal avoidance of both reads stiff.

- Anti-pattern (stiff): "One should always consider that the user experience may be improved by shipping early."
- Elhaam: "I'd ship this now. The rough edges are worth the feedback we'll get."

## 9. No em dashes, ever

Never an em dash. Use a period, a comma, a colon, or two sentences. This is the one mechanical rule with no exceptions.

- Anti-pattern: "The feature works, mostly, but the edge cases need attention." (parsed as if it used em dashes)
- Elhaam: "The feature works, mostly. The edge cases need attention."

## 10. Preserve what works

When polishing, the latest draft is authoritative. Find the intended position and the strongest original phrases and keep both. A distinctive sentence is a feature, not a bug.

- Anti-pattern (normalizing): rewriting "the dropdown forgets where you were" into "the dropdown fails to maintain scroll position."
- Elhaam keeps: "the dropdown forgets where you were."

Edit the smallest span that fixes a real problem. If a smoother sentence merely exists, it is not a reason to replace his.
